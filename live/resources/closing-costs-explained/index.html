Closing Costs Explained: Where Every Dollar Goes and How Much Cash You Really Need

September 12, 2026
Pylon

A first-time buyer may spend years saving a down payment and still be surprised by the amount required at closing.

That is because the down payment and the final cash to close are not the same number.

Imagine you are buying a $1,000,000 home with a $150,000 down payment and an $850,000 mortgage.

It is tempting to assume you will wire exactly $150,000.

But the transaction may also include lender charges, title and settlement fees, taxes, homeowners insurance, prepaid interest, escrow deposits, recording costs, credits, and a deposit you already paid when signing the contract.

The final amount is a reconciliation of all of those pieces.

Closing costs are not one fee. They are the financial footprint of originating, verifying, insuring, transferring, financing, and recording the purchase.

Start with the basic purchase math

At the simplest level:

  • Purchase price: $1,000,000

  • Mortgage proceeds: $850,000

  • Borrower down payment: $150,000

But a settlement statement has to account for far more than the purchase price.

A simplified cash-to-close equation is:

Down payment + closing costs + prepaid items + initial escrow deposits − deposits already paid − seller credits − lender credits − other applicable credits

That is why the final wire may be higher or lower than the planned down payment.

The five major categories

Most borrower-side closing figures fit into five broad groups:

  1. Down payment

  2. Loan and service costs

  3. Taxes, insurance, and other prepaid items

  4. Escrow funding

  5. Credits and prior payments

Understanding the categories is more useful than memorizing a list of fee names.

1. The down payment

The down payment is the portion of the purchase price not financed through the mortgage.

In the example:

$1,000,000 purchase price − $850,000 loan = $150,000 down payment

The down payment creates the borrower’s initial equity, subject to transaction costs and later changes in property value.

It also affects the loan-to-value ratio.

For an $850,000 loan on a $1,000,000 property, the approximate LTV is 85%.

The down payment may affect:

  • Product eligibility

  • Mortgage insurance

  • Pricing

  • Reserves

  • Monthly payment

  • Appraisal sensitivity

  • Cash remaining after closing

More down is not automatically better. A borrower should consider how much liquidity remains for repairs, moving, emergencies, and normal life after the transaction.

2. Lender-related charges

Lender charges may include compensation for originating, processing, underwriting, or pricing the loan.

Possible items include:

Origination charge

A lender fee for creating and managing the loan.

Underwriting or processing fees

Charges associated with evaluating and preparing the file, depending on the lender’s fee structure.

Discount points

Money paid upfront for a lower interest rate.

One point generally represents 1% of the loan amount.

On an $850,000 mortgage:

One point = $8,500

Paying points can reduce the monthly payment, but the borrower should calculate how long it takes for the monthly savings to recover the upfront cost.

Rate-lock or extension charges

A longer lock or an extension may carry a cost depending on the agreement and market conditions.

Lender credits

A lender credit reduces some upfront costs, often in exchange for different pricing such as a higher interest rate.

There is no magical disappearance of cost. There is usually a trade between money paid now and money paid over time.

3. Third-party loan services

The mortgage process relies on outside providers.

Possible charges include:

  • Appraisal

  • Credit report

  • Flood determination

  • Tax service

  • Employment or asset verification

  • Other required settlement or verification services

These charges are not necessarily lender profit. They may reimburse companies performing specific work.

4. Title and settlement costs

Title and settlement providers help establish the legal transfer and coordinate the closing.

Possible charges include:

  • Title search

  • Lender’s title insurance

  • Owner’s title insurance

  • Settlement or closing fee

  • Attorney fees where applicable

  • Endorsements

  • Municipal or lien searches

  • Document preparation

  • Recording coordination

Lender’s title insurance

Generally protects the lender’s lien interest against certain covered title problems.

Owner’s title insurance

Generally protects the homeowner’s ownership interest against certain covered title problems, subject to the policy.

These are different protections.

5. Government and recording charges

The transaction may include government-imposed or recording-related amounts, such as:

  • Deed recording fees

  • Mortgage or security-instrument recording fees

  • Transfer taxes or charges

  • Mortgage-related taxes in applicable jurisdictions

  • Local filing charges

These vary significantly by location and transaction.

A broad national estimate is not a substitute for property-specific calculations.

6. Homeowners insurance

The borrower may need to pay some or all of the first insurance premium at or before closing.

The lender also verifies that coverage is effective on time and reflects the correct property and mortgagee information.

Insurance appears in several possible places in the transaction:

  • Premium paid directly before closing

  • Premium collected at closing

  • Initial escrow deposit for future premiums

These should not be confused with one another.

7. Prepaid interest

Mortgage payments are commonly structured so the first regular payment is not due immediately at closing.

Prepaid interest covers the interest from the funding date through the end of that month.

The amount therefore depends partly on the closing date.

Closing earlier in a month can produce more days of prepaid interest than closing near month-end, although the timing of the first regular payment also changes.

This is a timing item, not simply a junk fee.

8. Property taxes

Taxes can affect the closing in several ways:

  • Proration between buyer and seller

  • Taxes already paid by the seller

  • Taxes currently due

  • Initial escrow deposit

  • Local transfer or mortgage taxes

The borrower is not expected to know every final tax number on the original application.

The lender may estimate early, then update the calculation when title, tax records, and the settlement company provide better information.

9. Initial escrow deposits

An escrow account allows the loan servicer to collect part of the expected property taxes and insurance with the monthly mortgage payment, then pay those bills when due.

At closing, money may be collected to establish the account.

This is not the same as a permanent fee.

It is money being held for future bills.

The escrow amount may include a number of months of:

  • Property taxes

  • Homeowners insurance

  • Mortgage insurance where applicable

The exact amount depends on due dates, closing timing, and the escrow calculation.

10. Mortgage insurance

Depending on the product and down payment, mortgage insurance may apply.

It may be:

  • Monthly

  • Upfront

  • Financed into the loan in some structures

  • A combination of charges depending on the product

Mortgage insurance protects the lender or investor against certain losses. It does not replace homeowners insurance and does not protect the borrower from losing the home after default.

11. Homeowners-association and property charges

For a condominium, cooperative, or property in an association, the closing may include:

  • Transfer fees

  • Move-in fees

  • Dues adjustments

  • Questionnaire charges

  • Capital contributions

  • Other association-specific items

Some charges may not be lender-controlled but still affect the total transaction.

12. Seller credits

The seller may agree to pay certain eligible buyer costs, subject to the contract and loan requirements.

A seller credit can reduce the borrower’s cash to close.

It does not usually become unrestricted cash paid to the borrower.

It must be reflected in the contract and final settlement figures and used within applicable limits.

13. Lender credits

A lender credit also reduces eligible upfront costs.

The tradeoff may be a higher rate or different loan pricing.

A useful comparison should show:

  • Credit received

  • Rate difference

  • Monthly payment difference

  • Approximate break-even period

  • Expected time the borrower will keep the loan

14. Earnest-money or contract deposit

A deposit paid after signing the purchase contract may already be held by an escrow, attorney, or other authorized party.

At closing, that amount is credited toward the borrower’s required contribution.

If the borrower has already paid $50,000 toward the transaction, the final wire should not simply add another full $150,000 down payment without recognizing that credit.

15. Prorations and adjustments

Some costs are divided between buyer and seller based on timing.

Examples may include:

  • Property taxes

  • Fuel

  • Rents

  • Association dues

  • Utilities or other local items

These adjustments help ensure each party pays or receives the appropriate amount for the period of ownership.

A simplified example

Consider this fictional transaction:

  • Purchase price: $1,000,000

  • Loan amount: $850,000

  • Down payment: $150,000

  • Lender and third-party costs: $12,000

  • Title and settlement costs: $9,000

  • Prepaids and initial escrows: $18,000

  • Recording and government charges: $6,000

  • Earnest-money deposit already paid: $50,000

  • Seller credit: $10,000

  • Lender credit: $5,000

Simplified cash to close:

$150,000 + $12,000 + $9,000 + $18,000 + $6,000 − $50,000 − $10,000 − $5,000

Estimated cash to close: $130,000

The borrower is still making a $150,000 down payment. The final additional wire is lower because some money was already deposited and credits offset costs.

This is only an illustration. Real transactions contain more detailed calculations.

Where the lender’s money goes

The lender does not normally deposit $850,000 into the borrower’s personal account.

Instead:

  • The lender sends loan proceeds into the settlement process.

  • The borrower sends cash to close.

  • The settlement agent distributes funds to the seller and other approved recipients.

Recipients may include:

  • Seller

  • Existing mortgage holders

  • Tax authorities

  • Real-estate brokers

  • Title provider

  • Insurer

  • Government recording office

  • Other parties listed in the settlement figures

The closing is both a document exchange and a controlled money-distribution event.

Loan Estimate versus Closing Disclosure

The Loan Estimate shows estimated loan terms and closing costs early in the process.

The Closing Disclosure shows the final or near-final figures before closing.

The borrower should compare them carefully.

Important questions include:

  • Did the loan amount change?

  • Did the interest rate change?

  • Are points or credits as expected?

  • Did title or government costs change?

  • Are seller credits included?

  • Is the prior deposit included?

  • Is the escrow structure correct?

  • Does the cash to close match the latest expectation?

A difference is not automatically an error, but it should be explainable.

Why final numbers move

Closing costs can change because:

  • The closing date changes

  • Prepaid-interest days change

  • Taxes are updated

  • Insurance premium changes

  • Title provides final fees

  • The contract is amended

  • Seller or lender credits change

  • The loan amount or product changes

  • The rate is repriced

  • Escrow calculations are finalized

A modern platform should show the reason for each material change instead of replacing one PDF with another and forcing the borrower to play financial archaeology.

How much should a buyer budget?

There is no universal percentage that accurately predicts every transaction.

Costs depend on:

  • Location

  • Purchase price

  • Loan product

  • Loan amount

  • Property type

  • Taxes

  • Insurance

  • Title practice

  • Government charges

  • Points or credits

  • Closing date

The safest planning method is to maintain three separate cash numbers:

  1. Planned down payment

  2. Estimated closing and prepaid costs

  3. Emergency reserves remaining after closing

The third number is the one buyers most often forget.

Wire fraud warning

Closing wires are a major target for fraud.

Before sending money:

  • Verify instructions using a trusted phone number obtained independently

  • Speak directly with the settlement provider

  • Confirm the recipient and account details

  • Treat last-minute changes as suspicious

  • Do not rely solely on an email thread

A beautiful closing statement does not make fraudulent wire instructions safe.

The Pylon model

Closing costs should not appear as a final surprise hidden in a static disclosure.

The Command Center should show:

  • Current estimated cash to close

  • What changed

  • Who supplied each number

  • Which figures are estimated or final

  • Seller and lender credits

  • Deposit already paid

  • Title balance status

  • Closing Disclosure readiness

  • Whether any missing amount blocks closing

The core question is simple:

How much must I send, where does it go, and why?

A modern mortgage platform should always have an answer.

The simplest takeaway

Your down payment buys equity.

Closing costs pay for the work, protections, taxes, and legal execution around the transaction.

Prepaids cover expenses that begin at closing.

Escrows fund future tax and insurance bills.

Credits and deposits reduce the amount still due.

All of it comes together in one final number:

Cash to close.
