Who Does What During a Mortgage? Every Person, System, and Handoff Explained

September 8, 2026
Pylon

Most borrowers believe they are working with one person: the loan officer.

Then the emails begin.

A processor asks for a bank statement. An appraiser wants access to the property. An underwriter creates a condition. A title officer asks about the seller. A closer sends final instructions. After closing, an entirely different company may collect the monthly payment.

It can feel as if the loan keeps being passed around because nobody owns it.

In reality, each participant owns a different kind of question.

The mortgage process is a relay race involving the borrower, lender, property professionals, settlement providers, investors, and software systems. The problem is not that many people are involved. The problem is that borrowers are rarely told how the pieces fit together.

Here is the map.

The borrower: the source of the original facts

The borrower is the person requesting the money and agreeing to repay it.

The borrower is responsible for:

  • Providing truthful application information

  • Authorizing credit and verification

  • Choosing among available loan options

  • Supplying required evidence

  • Answering legitimate follow-up questions

  • Reviewing disclosures

  • Signing closing documents

  • Providing the required cash to close through verified instructions

A well-run mortgage should not turn the borrower into an unpaid project manager.

The borrower’s job is primarily to provide, choose, approve, sign, and respond when a real exception requires attention.

When no action is required, the system should say so plainly.

The loan officer: the borrower’s guide and strategist

The loan officer, often shortened to LO, is usually the borrower’s main relationship with the lender or mortgage provider.

The LO helps:

  • Understand the borrower’s goal

  • Review the initial scenario

  • Discuss eligible products

  • Compare estimated payments and costs

  • Structure the loan

  • Explain tradeoffs

  • Set expectations

  • Communicate throughout the process

  • Help solve exceptions

The loan officer usually does not make the final underwriting decision.

That distinction matters.

The LO helps determine the most appropriate route. The underwriter decides whether the evidence proves the loan satisfies the route’s rules.

The future should make the LO more valuable, not less. Software can classify documents and monitor deadlines. The LO remains the human who understands the borrower’s priorities, explains difficult choices, and protects trust.

The loan officer is still the pilot. Better software gives the pilot better instruments.

The customer-side processor: the partner team’s operational owner

In embedded or partner lending, the organization serving the customer may have its own processor.

This person may:

  • Help the borrower complete the file

  • Monitor document requests

  • Coordinate with the LO

  • Track conditions

  • Communicate with agents

  • Escalate issues

  • Watch the closing date

The customer-side processor often needs transparency into work that is actually being performed by the lender’s internal operations team.

That is why a shared Command Center matters. Without it, both sides keep separate checklists and reconcile them through email.

The lender’s internal processor: the builder of the loan file

The internal processor converts the borrower’s application into a complete, reviewable loan file.

This role is responsible for operational completeness.

The processor may:

  • Generate document requests

  • Review whether the file contains all required items

  • Identify missing pages or expired evidence

  • Compare application facts with documents

  • Coordinate employment, income, and asset verification

  • Order or monitor third-party services

  • Prepare the loan for underwriting

  • Help clear underwriting conditions

The processor does not merely collect PDFs.

A good processor determines whether the evidence actually answers the required question.

A bank statement may arrive, but if it contains an unexplained deposit, the funds requirement may still be unresolved. A pay statement may be readable but too old. An insurance binder may exist but contain the wrong lender information.

Receipt is not the same as sufficiency.

The underwriter: the credit decision owner

The underwriter evaluates the complete borrower, property, product, and transaction.

The underwriter asks:

  • Is the borrower eligible?

  • Is the income acceptable and properly calculated?

  • Are assets sufficient and appropriately sourced?

  • Are all relevant debts included?

  • Does the credit profile satisfy the applicable requirements?

  • Does the property support the transaction?

  • Are the product and loan terms eligible?

  • Can the loan be closed and delivered without unacceptable risk?

The underwriter may return:

  • Approval

  • Conditional approval

  • Suspension pending information

  • Ineligibility for the selected product

  • Denial

The underwriter’s output should not be a mysterious verdict. It should be a traceable decision supported by rules, evidence, and clearly written conditions.

Automated underwriting systems: the first structured risk pass

Mortgage lenders frequently use automated underwriting systems to evaluate structured loan data.

These systems can produce findings about risk, eligibility, and documentation.

They do not eliminate the need to verify that the application data is accurate. A system can only evaluate the facts it receives.

If the application says the borrower earns $400,000, the lender must still prove that qualifying income through acceptable evidence.

A modern mortgage platform should connect the automated findings to the actual evidence instead of leaving them in separate files.

The post-lock account manager: the coordinator after pricing is locked

Once a rate is locked, the closing timeline becomes financially important.

A post-lock account manager may coordinate work among:

  • Processing

  • Underwriting

  • Appraisal

  • Title

  • Insurance

  • Disclosures

  • Closing

  • External partners

This is fundamentally an orchestration role.

Much of it can be handled by an intelligent agent that:

  • Watches every workstream

  • Assigns tasks

  • Follows up automatically

  • Updates predictions

  • Detects risk

  • Escalates only genuine exceptions

A human account manager should focus on situations requiring judgment, authority, negotiation, or relationship management—not spend the day manually asking whether a report arrived.

The appraiser: the property value expert

The appraiser is independent of the borrower’s qualification decision.

The appraiser’s primary task is to develop a supported opinion of the property’s value for the transaction.

The appraisal may also contain relevant observations about the property and comparable market evidence.

The appraiser does not decide whether the borrower can repay the loan.

The underwriter or collateral reviewer determines whether the completed valuation is acceptable for the selected loan.

An appraisal may move through statuses such as:

  • Ordered

  • Inspection scheduled

  • Inspection complete

  • Report received

  • Under review

  • Correction requested

  • Accepted

Because appraisal work often involves third-party scheduling, it can become part of the closing critical path.

The home inspector: the buyer’s condition expert

The home inspector and appraiser are not interchangeable.

The inspector helps the buyer understand the physical condition of the property.

The inspector may identify issues involving:

  • Roof

  • Structure

  • Plumbing

  • Electrical systems

  • Heating and cooling

  • Moisture

  • Safety

  • Appliances

The inspection is generally for the buyer’s decision-making and contractual protections.

The appraisal primarily serves the lending valuation process.

The title company or settlement agent: the ownership and money coordinator

The title or settlement provider helps answer:

  • Who owns the property now?

  • Can the seller legally transfer it?

  • What liens or claims exist?

  • What must be paid off?

  • What fees and taxes apply?

  • Can the lender obtain the required lien position?

  • How should the deed identify the new owner?

At closing, the settlement provider may also:

  • Balance figures with the lender

  • Receive borrower and lender funds

  • Pay the seller and other parties

  • Coordinate signatures

  • Submit documents for recording

  • Confirm disbursement and recording

The title company is not approving the borrower’s credit. It is protecting the legal transfer and settlement of the property transaction.

The insurance agent or carrier: the property protection source

The borrower usually selects homeowners insurance.

The insurance provider supplies the information the lender needs to verify acceptable coverage.

This may include:

  • Insured property

  • Named insured

  • Coverage amount

  • Premium

  • Effective date

  • Deductible

  • Mortgagee information

A binder can be corrected if information is wrong. That correction may seem administrative, but if acceptable coverage is not effective at closing, it can become a funding blocker.

The closer: the person who turns approval into executable documents

The closer works after underwriting has largely completed.

The closer may:

  • Confirm disclosure readiness

  • Coordinate final fees

  • Balance with title or settlement

  • Generate the closing package

  • Schedule or coordinate signing

  • Review final closing conditions

  • Prepare the loan for funding

The closer’s question is not, “Should we make this loan?”

It is:

Is the approved loan ready to be documented, signed, funded, and recorded correctly?

The signing agent or notary: the execution witness

Depending on local practice, a notary or signing agent may guide the borrower through execution of the closing package and notarize required signatures.

This person generally does not renegotiate the loan or make underwriting decisions.

If the borrower identifies an unexpected term, the correct response is to pause and contact the authorized loan or settlement team—not ask the notary to interpret or change the transaction.

The funder: the final money gate

The funding team checks whether the signed loan satisfies the conditions required before money is released.

The funder may confirm:

  • Required documents are signed

  • Notarizations are acceptable

  • Funding conditions are cleared

  • Wire instructions and settlement data are correct

  • The loan is authorized to disburse

Only then are the loan proceeds released through the settlement process.

The seller and the seller’s team

The seller is transferring the property.

The seller and representatives may be responsible for:

  • Signing the purchase contract and amendments

  • Providing property and title information

  • Resolving liens or payoff issues

  • Completing required repairs or agreements

  • Signing the deed and seller documents

  • Delivering possession under the contract

The borrower’s lender cannot control every seller-side delay, but a good orchestration system should show its impact.

Real-estate agents and attorneys

Real-estate agents help negotiate and coordinate the purchase transaction.

Attorneys may play major roles depending on the state and local process, including contract review, title, closing, legal advice, and settlement.

These professionals are not substitutes for the lender’s underwriting and closing teams. They manage different legal and transactional responsibilities.

The lender of record, capital provider, and investor

These roles may be one company—or several.

Lender or originator

The entity making or originating the mortgage under the applicable arrangement.

Warehouse or capital provider

The source that may temporarily finance the loan before it is sold or delivered.

Investor or loan owner

The party that ultimately owns the loan asset or acquires an interest in it.

Servicer

The company that collects payments and manages the ongoing loan account after closing.

The company that originates the loan may not keep it for thirty years. The owner of the loan may not be the company that sends the monthly statement.

These distinctions explain why underwriting and data quality matter beyond closing. The loan may need to satisfy requirements for delivery into capital markets.

Where Pylon fits

Pylon is not one of the human roles.

Pylon is the infrastructure and orchestration layer connecting them.

It can support the journey from:

Application → Product and pricing → Requirements → Evidence → Underwriting → Conditions → Disclosures → Closing → Funding → Capital markets

The opportunity is to create one shared loan state rather than force each team to maintain its own interpretation.

For each stage, the Command Center should show:

  • What must be true

  • What is already proven

  • What remains missing

  • Which evidence supports the conclusion

  • Who owns the next action

  • Whether the item blocks closing

  • The predicted completion date

  • Confidence that the target closing date will be met

Why handoffs fail

Handoffs fail when the object being transferred is merely a document or task, not an understood conclusion.

The processor says the document was uploaded.

The underwriter says the requirement is not satisfied.

The LO tells the borrower it was already completed.

All three may be describing different states.

A modern system needs explicit states such as:

  • Requested

  • Received

  • Classified

  • Reviewed

  • Verified

  • Accepted for a specific requirement

  • Rejected

  • Superseded

  • Expired

That is how a relay race becomes an operating system.

The simplest responsibility map

The borrower provides and approves.

The loan officer advises and communicates.

The processor builds and verifies the file.

The underwriter decides.

The appraiser supports value.

Title protects the ownership transfer and lien.

Insurance protects the property.

The closer prepares execution.

The funder releases money.

The recorder makes the deed and lien public record.

Capital markets finance and own the loan after origination.

Pylon connects the whole system.
